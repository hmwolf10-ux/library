4. Cost Curves — The Heart of Production Theory

Every firm faces a set of cost curves. These determine when it's profitable to produce, how much to produce, and when to shut down. Knowing how to read these is fundamental to understanding firm behavior.

All Curves

Key rules: (1) MC always crosses AVC and ATC at their minimum points. (2) ATC - AVC = AFC (the vertical gap narrows as quantity rises — fixed costs spread out). (3) When MC < ATC, ATC is falling. When MC > ATC, ATC is rising. This is pure math — not econ intuition.

ATC (Average Total Cost)Total cost ÷ quantityU-shaped, above AVC by exactly AFC
MC (Marginal Cost)Cost of producing one more unitU-shaped, crosses AVC and ATC at their minimums

5. Profit Maximization — MR = MC

Every firm maximizes profit by producing the quantity where Marginal Revenue = Marginal Cost. If MR > MC, produce more (each unit adds to profit). If MR < MC, produce less (each unit costs more than it earns). This rule applies to ALL market structures.

Short-run shutdown ruleIf P < AVC at the optimal Q, it's cheaper to produce zero (you still lose fixed costs but avoid losing more on variable costs).
Long-run exit ruleIf P < ATC, exit the market in the long run (once fixed costs can be avoided).

6. Market Structures

The four structures differ in number of firms, product differentiation, and pricing power. This determines whether consumers get a good deal or get exploited.

In perfect competition, P = MR = MC in equilibrium. In a monopoly, P > MR = MC — the monopolist restricts output to raise price, creating deadweight loss. This gap between P and MC is the measure of monopoly power (Lerner Index).

7. Deadweight Loss

Deadweight loss (DWL) is the value destroyed when markets don't reach the efficient quantity. It's the "missing triangle" — trades that would have been mutually beneficial but didn't happen because of a tax, price control, or monopoly restriction.

8. Externalities — When Markets Fail

Externalities are costs or benefits that fall on people outside the transaction. The market ignores them, so it produces the wrong quantity. Government intervention (taxes, subsidies, regulation) can correct this.
