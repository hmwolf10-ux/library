1. Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow — you can invest it. This single idea underlies every valuation, bond price, mortgage payment, and investment decision ever made. PV and FV are two sides of the same equation.

2. DCF Valuation & Sensitivity

A company is worth the present value of all its future cash flows, discounted back to today. DCF is theoretically pure — and practically treacherous. Small changes in discount rate or growth rate swing the value dramatically.

Cash Flow Waterfall
Rate Sensitivity
Terminal Value Weight

Why DCF is hard in practice: A 1% change in discount rate can change value by 20–40%. A 0.5% change in terminal growth rate can swing value by 15%. Most of the value (often 60–80%) sits in the "terminal value" — a number calculated beyond the forecast horizon. Garbage in, garbage out. Always look at multiple methods (DCF + comps + precedent transactions).

3. WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital

WACC is the discount rate used in DCF — the minimum return a company must earn to satisfy both debt holders and equity holders. Higher WACC = lower valuation. This is why rising interest rates kill valuations: they raise WACC.

4. Optimal Capital Structure

How much debt vs equity should a company use? Debt is cheaper (tax shield) but adds financial distress risk. There's an optimal mix that minimizes WACC and maximizes firm value — and it shifts by industry, economic cycle, and firm maturity.

The U-shaped WACC curve: As leverage increases, WACC initially falls (cheap debt + tax shield). But beyond the optimal point, financial distress costs rise, equity holders demand higher returns, and cost of debt spikes — WACC rises. The goal: find the trough. Trade-off theory says this optimal point exists. Modigliani-Miller says in a perfect market, it doesn't matter (capital structure irrelevance) — but markets aren't perfect, so it does matter.

5. Break-Even Analysis

At what sales volume does revenue exactly cover total costs? Below break-even = losses. Above = profit. Critical for any business decision: launching a product, opening a location, making a capital investment.

Margin of Safety: How far can sales fall from your current level before you reach break-even? If you're selling 5,000 units and break-even is 2,500, margin of safety = 50%. Higher margin of safety = more resilient business. Contribution Margin = (Price − VC) = how much each sale contributes toward covering fixed costs.

8. DuPont Analysis — Decomposing ROE

Return on Equity (ROE) is the ultimate profitability metric for equity holders. DuPont breaks it into three (or five) drivers, showing WHY ROE is high or low — and whether it's sustainable or risky.

3-Factor DuPont
Company Comparison
5-Factor DuPont

ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Financial Leverage
Net Margin = Pricing power, cost control | Asset Turnover = Efficiency (how hard assets work) | Financial Leverage = D/E ratio (amplifier — cuts both ways)

Two companies can have the same ROE via completely different paths. Amazon: high turnover, thin margins. Apple: high margins, moderate turnover. A bank: high leverage. Understanding which lever drives ROE tells you if it's sustainable.
