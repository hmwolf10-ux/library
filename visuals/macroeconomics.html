Macroeconomics

How entire economies behave — GDP, inflation, unemployment, interest rates, and the government and central bank tools that try to manage them.

2. The Business Cycle — GDP Over Time

3. Phillips Curve — Inflation vs. Unemployment Trade-off

The empirical relationship between inflation and unemployment — one of the most debated charts in all of economics. Low unemployment → tight labor markets → wage pressure → inflation. The Fed must balance both sides (the "dual mandate").

Short-Run PC
Long-Run PC (Vertical)
Stagflation Shift
Expectations-Augmented

The 1970s breakdown: The Phillips Curve appeared to shift outward — both inflation AND unemployment were high simultaneously ("stagflation"). Caused by oil price shocks (cost-push inflation) plus entrenched inflationary expectations. Paul Volcker solved it 1979–82 by raising rates to 20%, accepting a severe recession to re-anchor expectations.

7. The Laffer Curve

Shows the relationship between tax rates and government tax revenue. At 0% or 100%, revenue is zero. Somewhere between, revenue is maximized. The key debate: what tax rate maximizes revenue, and what rate is currently "too high"? Politicians on both sides weaponize this without data.

Key insight: The curve itself is not controversial — it's math. The debate is empirical: is the current tax rate on the left side (raising taxes = more revenue) or right side (cutting taxes = more revenue)? Most economists think US income tax rates are on the left side of the curve (raising rates would increase revenue). Supply-siders argue otherwise. The top income tax rate in the US was 91% in the 1950s and still generated strong growth — evidence the curve's peak is likely well above current US rates.

8. Okun's Law — GDP Growth vs. Unemployment

Empirical relationship: for every 1% increase in unemployment above the natural rate, GDP is roughly 2% below potential. When the economy is growing, unemployment falls. When it contracts, unemployment rises — but with a lag.

Okun's coefficient ≈ 2: For every 1 percentage point rise in unemployment, real GDP falls about 2% below potential. Modern estimates range from 1.5× to 2.5×, varying by country and time period. The relationship isn't perfectly stable — labor hoarding, part-time work, and productivity changes all add noise.

9. The Fiscal Multiplier

When the government spends $1, how much does GDP rise by? If the multiplier is 1.5, $1 of spending creates $1.50 of GDP. If it's 0.8, it creates less than $1. The multiplier varies wildly with economic conditions — highest during a recession (lots of spare capacity), lowest during full employment (crowding out).

10. IS-LM Model

A more complete macro model that combines goods markets (IS curve) and money markets (LM curve). IS = investment-saving balance (goods market). LM = money supply-demand (money market). Where they cross = simultaneous equilibrium in both markets — the economy's short-run equilibrium.
