A Reference Guide to Thinking Clearly

"The first rule is that you can't really know anything if you just remember isolated facts and try and bang 'em back. If the facts don't hang together on a latticework of theory, you don't have them in a usable form."

— Charlie Munger

Thinking Frameworks

The foundational mental models for reasoning clearly. Start here.

MODELFirst Principles Thinking
OriginPhysics / Aristotle
In shortBreak everything down to its fundamental truths and reason up from there — don't copy what already exists.
How it worksInstead of reasoning by analogy ("we'll do it like they do it"), you decompose a problem to its irreducible basics and rebuild from scratch. Elon Musk famously used this to figure out that batteries don't have to cost what they cost — the raw materials were far cheaper than the assembled pack.
Use it whenYou're stuck in "how it's always been done." You want to innovate, not iterate. You're trying to price or build something from scratch.
MODELInversion
OriginMathematics / Charlie Munger
In shortInstead of asking how to succeed, ask how to guarantee failure — then avoid those things.
How it worksMunger borrowed this from the mathematician Jacobi: "Invert, always invert." Rather than asking "how do I build a great company?", ask "what would definitely destroy a company?" The answers — dishonesty, poor incentives, ignoring customers — become your checklist to avoid.
Use it whenYou're planning something important. You're overconfident. You want to stress-test a strategy or decision before committing.
MODELSecond-Order Thinking
OriginSystems Theory / Howard Marks
In shortAsk not just what happens next, but what happens because of what happens next.
How it worksFirst-order thinking: "If I fire this person, I save money." Second-order: "...but morale drops, two others quit, and I spend more rehiring." Most people stop at first-order. Marks built Oaktree Capital largely on the insight that markets price first-order effects quickly — alpha comes from thinking further.
Use it whenMaking any consequential decision. Evaluating a policy, investment, or major life change. Anywhere the obvious answer might be wrong because of downstream effects.
MODELThe Map Is Not the Territory
OriginAlfred Korzybski / General Semantics
In shortYour mental model of reality is not reality itself — and confusing the two is the source of most serious errors.
How it worksAll models, frameworks, and theories are simplifications. They're useful — like a map — but they leave things out. The danger is treating the map as if it were the actual terrain. In finance: a DCF model is not the company. In life: your opinion of someone is not the person.
Use it whenYou're relying heavily on a model or framework. You're surprised by an outcome your model predicted differently. You're debating someone who seems to be arguing with your frame rather than reality.
MODELOccam's Razor
OriginWilliam of Ockham, 14th century
In shortAmong competing explanations, prefer the simplest one that fits the facts.
How it worksWhen multiple hypotheses explain the same data, the one with the fewest assumptions is probably correct. This doesn't mean simple explanations are always right — it means complexity needs justification. In investing: if a company's earnings can be explained by legitimate growth or by fraud, start with legitimate growth unless evidence says otherwise.
Use it whenYou're diagnosing a problem. You're evaluating competing theories or narratives. You're resisting the temptation to over-explain.
MODELHanlon's Razor
OriginRobert J. Hanlon
In shortNever attribute to malice what can be adequately explained by stupidity — or more charitably, by ignorance.
How it worksMost bad outcomes aren't the result of conspiracy or intent — they're incompetence, miscommunication, or bad systems. This matters enormously in business: before assuming your colleague sabotaged you, consider whether they just didn't understand what you needed.
Use it whenYou feel wronged or undermined. You're tempted to assume bad intent. You're managing people or navigating office politics.
MODELBayes' Theorem
OriginThomas Bayes / Statistics
In shortUpdate your beliefs based on new evidence — proportionally to how much that evidence actually matters.
How it worksYou have a prior belief (e.g., "this stock is overvalued"). New information arrives (earnings beat). Bayes says: update your belief proportionally to how surprising that evidence is and how reliable the source is. Most people either ignore new evidence (anchoring) or overreact to it (recency bias). Bayesian thinking is the middle path.
Use it whenYou receive new information that contradicts your current view. You're making probabilistic decisions. You want to hold beliefs with appropriate confidence — not too tight, not too loose.

Cognitive Biases

The bugs in your mental operating system. Knowing them doesn't make you immune — but it helps.

MODELConfirmation Bias
OriginPsychology / Cognitive Science
In shortWe seek out, remember, and favor information that confirms what we already believe.
How it worksThe most pervasive bias in human cognition. We unconsciously filter the world to validate our existing beliefs. In investing, this means you'll find 12 reasons your thesis is right and ignore the 3 that say it's wrong. The fix: actively seek out the strongest case against your position before committing.
Use it whenYou've formed a strong opinion. You're researching an investment or decision. You notice you've only been reading things that agree with you.
MODELAvailability Heuristic
OriginKahneman & Tversky
In shortWe judge the probability of events by how easily examples come to mind — not by actual frequency.
How it worksAfter a plane crash, people wildly overestimate the danger of flying. After a bull market, people underestimate risk. Whatever is vivid, recent, or emotionally salient feels more likely than it statistically is. This is why markets panic and why people fear the wrong things.
Use it whenYou're assessing risk. You've just seen something dramatic in the news. You're trying to estimate how common something actually is.
MODELAnchoring
OriginKahneman & Tversky
In shortWe over-rely on the first piece of information we receive when making decisions.
How it worksYou see a watch listed at $500, marked down to $200. The $500 is an anchor — and it makes $200 feel like a deal, even if the watch is worth $80. In salary negotiations, whoever states a number first anchors the conversation. In valuation, the first model you build anchors your thinking about what's reasonable.
Use it whenNegotiating anything. Valuing a business or asset. Evaluating a deal that has been framed by someone else first.
MODELSunk Cost Fallacy
OriginBehavioral Economics
In shortWe continue investing in something because of what we've already spent — not because of future expected value.
How it works"We've already spent $2M on this project, we can't stop now." That $2M is gone regardless. The only question is whether continuing creates more value than the next best use of new resources. This is one of the most costly cognitive errors in business — it keeps failing projects, relationships, and strategies alive far past their expiration date.
Use it whenYou're reconsidering a commitment that isn't working. Someone argues for continuing because of past investment. You feel reluctant to quit something you've spent a lot on.
MODELDunning-Kruger Effect
OriginDavid Dunning & Justin Kruger, 1999
In shortPeople with limited knowledge in a domain overestimate their competence; experts often underestimate theirs.
How it worksBeginners don't know enough to know what they don't know — so they feel confident. As you learn more, you realize the depth of what you don't understand, and confidence dips. True expertise brings calibrated confidence back. In finance: the most dangerous investor is the one who made money in a bull market and thinks they're a genius.
Use it whenYou're evaluating your own expertise. You're assessing advice from someone who seems very confident. You've recently learned something new and feel certain about it.
MODELLoss Aversion
OriginKahneman & Tversky
In shortLosses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good — and this distorts almost every decision.
How it worksLosing $100 feels about twice as bad as winning $100 feels good. This asymmetry causes people to hold losing investments too long (avoiding realizing the loss), to take too little risk with upside, and to make poor deals out of fear of losing something they already have. Munger called it "the most important thing in behavioral economics."
Use it whenYou're holding a losing position. You're negotiating from a defensive posture. You're evaluating a risk/reward tradeoff and your gut says no despite favorable odds.
MODELSocial Proof
OriginRobert Cialdini
In shortWe look to what others are doing as a signal of correct behavior — especially in uncertain situations.
How it worksSocial proof is a useful heuristic in low-stakes situations. In investing and markets, it's catastrophic — it's literally the mechanism of bubbles. Everyone buying signals it's good; the crowd validates itself into collapse. Understanding this doesn't make you immune, but it makes you more likely to pause when something is popular purely because it's popular.
Use it whenAn investment thesis is based largely on what smart people are doing. You feel peer pressure to act. You're evaluating something that's gone viral or become consensus.

Systems Thinking

How complex systems behave — and why interventions so often backfire.

MODELFeedback Loops
OriginSystems Theory / Cybernetics
In shortOutputs of a system loop back as inputs — either amplifying (positive) or stabilizing (negative) the system.
How it worksPositive feedback loops amplify: a stock rises, more people buy, it rises more (bubble). Negative feedback loops stabilize: a thermostat detects heat and turns off the furnace. Most complex systems — markets, companies, bodies — are governed by both types simultaneously. Ignoring feedback loops is why interventions have unintended consequences.
Use it whenDesigning any system or process. Trying to understand why a problem keeps recurring. Evaluating policy or organizational change.
MODELMargin of Safety
OriginBenjamin Graham / Engineering
In shortBuild in a buffer between your assumptions and reality to protect against being wrong.
How it worksEngineers design bridges to hold 3x the maximum expected load — not because they expect that load, but because assumptions can be wrong. Graham applied this to investing: buy at enough of a discount that even if your analysis is wrong, you don't lose much. The wider the margin, the more errors you can absorb.
Use it whenMaking any irreversible decision. Valuing an asset. Building a financial model. Anywhere your estimates could be wrong and the consequences of being wrong are severe.
MODELRegression to the Mean
OriginFrancis Galton / Statistics
In shortExtreme outcomes tend to be followed by more average ones — because extreme results usually include a large element of luck.
How it worksThe best-performing fund this year is unlikely to be the best next year. The worst-performing quarter is unlikely to repeat. Extreme outliers in any direction contain a luck component that won't persist. This is why sports teams that overpay for a star year often get disappointment — and why companies that panic after one bad quarter often recover.
Use it whenEvaluating performance over short time periods. Deciding whether to continue or exit after an extreme result. Distinguishing skill from luck.
MODELThe Pareto Principle (80/20)
OriginVilfredo Pareto / Economics
In shortRoughly 80% of outcomes come from 20% of causes.
How it works80% of revenue from 20% of customers. 80% of bugs from 20% of code. 80% of your results from 20% of your effort. The distribution isn't always exactly 80/20, but the underlying insight — that inputs and outputs are almost never uniformly distributed — is one of the most practical in business.
Use it whenPrioritizing where to focus. Diagnosing why something isn't working. Deciding what to cut or double down on.
MODELNetwork Effects
OriginEconomics / Technology
In shortA product or service becomes more valuable as more people use it — creating a compounding competitive advantage.
How it worksThe telephone is useless with one user, invaluable with a billion. Visa, Airbnb, Excel, and LinkedIn all have network effects. This creates winner-take-most dynamics and near-impenetrable moats. In investing, identifying a true network effect early is one of the highest-value things you can do.
Use it whenEvaluating a business's competitive position. Assessing why certain companies seem impossible to displace. Building a product strategy.
MODELCritical Mass / Tipping Points
OriginPhysics / Malcolm Gladwell / Epidemiology
In shortSystems often behave linearly until a threshold is crossed — then they change state rapidly and irreversibly.
How it worksWater doesn't gradually become steam — it crosses 100°C and transforms. Diseases don't spread gradually — they tip into epidemics. Companies don't grow linearly — they reach distribution tipping points. Recognizing that many systems have non-linear thresholds explains why things seem fine until suddenly they're not.
Use it whenAssessing market adoption of a new product. Evaluating financial risk (leverage can look fine until it doesn't). Understanding why trends or movements suddenly explode.
MODELIncentives
OriginEconomics / Charlie Munger
In shortShow me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome. People do what they are rewarded for — even unconsciously.
How it worksMunger considered this the most powerful force in human behavior, bar none. Doctors who get paid per procedure perform more procedures. Salespeople who get commission on volume sell volume over quality. Before trusting any advice, analysis, or recommendation — understand who benefits from you believing it.
Use it whenEvaluating any advice, research, or recommendation. Designing compensation or organizational structure. Trying to understand why an institution behaves the way it does.

Decision Making

Frameworks for making better choices under uncertainty.

MODELExpected Value
OriginMathematics / Probability
In shortMultiply each possible outcome by its probability, sum them — that's the decision's expected value.
How it worksA 50% chance of winning $200 and 50% chance of losing $100 has an EV of +$50. Positive EV decisions should be taken repeatedly even if they sometimes lose. The best investors think probabilistically — not "will this work?" but "what's the EV across many similar decisions?" Most people avoid this because a loss feels worse than the math suggests.
Use it whenMaking any decision with uncertain outcomes. Evaluating investments. Deciding whether to take a calculated risk.
MODELCircle of Competence
OriginWarren Buffett / Charlie Munger
In shortKnow what you know, know what you don't know, and stay inside the boundary.
How it worksBuffett famously passed on technology companies for decades — not because he thought they were bad, but because he couldn't reliably predict their futures. He knew the boundary of his competence and respected it. The danger isn't a small circle — it's not knowing where your circle ends.
Use it whenEvaluating investment opportunities outside your experience. Being asked for advice in an unfamiliar domain. Feeling pressure to have an opinion on everything.
MODELOpportunity Cost
OriginEconomics
In shortEvery choice forecloses other choices — the true cost of a decision includes what you give up.
How it worksBuying one stock means not buying another. Spending time on one project means not spending it on another. Working a job means not starting a company. Most people only see what they're getting — rarely what they're giving up. In capital allocation, Buffett calls opportunity cost the "alternative" — always compare to the best alternative use.
Use it whenAllocating capital, time, or attention. Evaluating whether to continue something. Any decision where resources are finite.
MODELReversibility
OriginJeff Bezos / Decision Theory
In shortDistinguish between two-way door decisions (easily reversed) and one-way door decisions (permanent).
How it worksBezos distinguishes Type 1 decisions (irreversible, high-stakes — take slowly) from Type 2 decisions (reversible, low-stakes — move fast). Most organizations apply Type 1 caution to Type 2 decisions, which kills speed. Apply maximum scrutiny where you can't go back. Move fast where you can.
Use it whenDeciding how much time to spend on a decision. Evaluating a major commitment (hiring, acquisition, strategy shift). Anywhere being wrong would be catastrophic vs. just inconvenient.
MODELPre-Mortem
OriginGary Klein / Psychology
In shortBefore a project starts, imagine it has already failed — then work backwards to figure out why.
How it worksInstead of asking "will this work?" after you're emotionally committed, ask "it's one year from now and this failed spectacularly — what happened?" This technique short-circuits optimism bias and surfaces risks that groupthink suppresses. Used by the best project teams, investors, and military planners.
Use it whenBefore launching a project, investment, or major initiative. When a team is too aligned and no one is raising concerns. When you want to find the failure modes before they find you.
MODELAsymmetry
OriginOptions Theory / Nassim Taleb
In shortSeek situations where the downside is capped and the upside is open-ended — avoid the reverse.
How it worksOptions have asymmetric payoffs — you can lose the premium but can't lose more; the upside is theoretically unlimited. Taleb built a career on identifying asymmetries. In life: read widely (small time cost, potentially enormous payoff). In business: experiments have limited downside if they fail and large upside if they work. Avoid situations where you can lose catastrophically but only gain modestly.
Use it whenEvaluating any risk/reward tradeoff. Deciding whether to experiment or commit. Assessing a negotiation or deal structure.

Economics & World Models

How markets, competition, and civilization actually work beneath the surface.

MODELComparative Advantage
OriginDavid Ricardo / Economics
In shortDo what you're relatively better at — even if someone else is absolutely better at everything.
How it worksRicardo proved mathematically that even if one country is better at producing everything, both countries benefit from specialization and trade. Applied to life: you should focus on what you do relatively better than the alternatives, not just what you're best at in absolute terms. This is why delegation works — even if you're better than your assistant at everything, their time is cheaper.
Use it whenDeciding where to focus your career or business. Evaluating whether to outsource or delegate. Thinking about what to specialize in.
MODELCreative Destruction
OriginJoseph Schumpeter / Economics
In shortCapitalism advances by constantly destroying old industries and creating new ones — disruption is the engine, not a bug.
How it worksSchumpeter argued that the essential fact about capitalism is this incessant destruction of the old and creation of the new. Buggy whip makers were destroyed by cars. Film photography by digital. This is why moats erode, why monopolies are temporary, and why the best investment question is always "what could kill this business?"
Use it whenEvaluating the long-term durability of a business model. Thinking about industry disruption. Assessing your own career's exposure to automation or obsolescence.
MODELGame Theory / Nash Equilibrium
OriginJohn Nash / Mathematics
In shortIn competitive situations, understand what happens when all parties act in their own rational self-interest simultaneously.
How it worksThe Nash Equilibrium is the point where no player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing strategy. Knowing this tells you what outcomes are stable — even if they're not optimal for everyone. The Prisoner's Dilemma shows how individually rational choices can produce collectively bad outcomes. Essential for negotiations, pricing strategy, and market competition.
Use it whenNegotiating against a sophisticated counterparty. Thinking about competitive strategy. Understanding why markets, industries, or negotiations reach certain stable states.
MODELMean Reversion vs. Momentum
OriginStatistics / Finance
In shortSome variables revert to average over time — others trend. Knowing which you're dealing with changes everything.
How it worksValuations mean-revert — expensive markets become cheaper, cheap markets become expensive. Earnings momentum tends to persist in the short run. Confusing the two is a major source of investment error. The question before any bet: is this a mean-reverting variable (bet against extremes) or a momentum variable (bet with the trend)?
Use it whenMaking any prediction about future performance. Evaluating whether a trend will continue or reverse. Assessing investment valuation vs. momentum dynamics.
MODELThe Lindy Effect
OriginNassim Taleb / Statistics
In shortFor non-perishable things, the longer something has survived, the longer it's likely to continue to survive.
How it worksA book that's been in print for 200 years will probably be in print for another 200. A company that's been around for 100 years has more institutional durability than a startup. Technology, ideas, and institutions that have survived stress-testing by time have demonstrated robustness. New things have not. This doesn't mean new is bad — it means be appropriately skeptical until time has proven otherwise.
Use it whenEvaluating the durability of an idea, technology, or institution. Deciding how much to trust something new vs. something time-tested. Building a reading list (Lindy-approved books are worth prioritizing).
MODELReflexivity
OriginGeorge Soros
In shortIn financial markets, participant beliefs don't just reflect reality — they change it, creating self-fulfilling and self-defeating cycles.
How it worksStandard economics assumes markets reflect reality. Soros argues the relationship is two-way: beliefs affect prices, and prices affect fundamentals, which affect beliefs again. A bank that people believe is failing faces a bank run — and fails. A stock everyone believes will rise attracts capital — and rises, justifying the belief. This feedback between perception and reality is the core of bubbles and crashes.
Use it whenUnderstanding market bubbles and manias. Analyzing why consensus views can persist even when wrong. Thinking about how narrative affects fundamentals (not just reflects them).

Where to go deeper

Poor Charlie's Almanack — Munger's collected speeches, the original source for lattice-of-models thinking.

Thinking, Fast and Slow — Kahneman's definitive work on cognitive bias and decision-making.

The Great Mental Models (4 vols.) — Shane Parrish's systematic treatment of the most important models.

Farnam Street (fs.blog) — The best ongoing source for mental models and decision-making writing on the internet.

Antifragile — Taleb's framework for systems that gain from disorder. The Lindy Effect and Asymmetry go much deeper here.
