Strategy is about how a business creates and sustains competitive advantage. Everything else — marketing, ops, finance — is in service of that.

Competitive Advantage — The Core Question

The fundamental question of strategy: why does this business earn returns above its cost of capital? The answer is competitive advantage (or "moat"). Without it, competition will erode returns to zero.

Types of Moats (Buffett's Framework)

Moat TypeHow It WorksExamplesDurability
Network EffectsProduct becomes more valuable as more people use it. Creates a self-reinforcing loop where the leader gets stronger as it grows.Visa, Mastercard, Meta, LinkedIn, OpenTable, exchangesVery high — often winner-take-most
Switching CostsCustomers are locked in by cost, effort, or risk of switching. Not just convenience — actual pain.Salesforce, Oracle, Workday, Bloomberg Terminal, SAP, Apple ecosystemHigh — but can erode if a competitor makes switching easy enough
Cost AdvantageStructural cost position that can't be replicated — from scale, proprietary process, unique assets, or location.Costco (no-frills), GEICO (direct model), Rio Tinto (low-cost mines)Moderate — technology can disrupt; scale requires continuous reinvestment
Intangible AssetsBrands, patents, licenses, regulatory approvals that competitors can't replicate.Coca-Cola (brand), Pfizer (patents), Goldman Sachs (reputation), Google (search brand)Variable — patents expire; brands erode without investment
Efficient ScaleMarket is large enough to support only a few players profitably. A new entrant would destroy both their own and incumbents' returns.Airports, pipelines, local utilities, niche B2B platformsHigh — natural oligopoly or monopoly dynamic
Moat vs. Quality: A business can have great products and management but no moat — returns will still erode over time. Look for structural reasons why competitors can't earn the same margins. Ask: "What stops a well-funded competitor from entering this market?"

Porter's Five Forces

Michael Porter's framework for analyzing industry profitability. The five forces determine the competitive intensity of any industry and why some industries are structurally more profitable than others.

ForceWhat It AsksStrong (Bad) = Low ReturnsWeak (Good) = High Returns
1. Threat of New EntrantsHow easy is it for new competitors to enter?Low barriers: easy to copy product, low capital required, no brand loyaltyHigh barriers: regulatory approval, high CapEx, network effects, brand, patents
2. Bargaining Power of SuppliersHow much power do suppliers have to raise prices?Concentrated suppliers, switching costs, critical inputsMany suppliers, commodity inputs, easy to switch
3. Bargaining Power of BuyersHow much power do customers have to push prices down?Few large customers, undifferentiated product, price-sensitiveMany fragmented customers, mission-critical product, high switching costs
4. Threat of SubstitutesCan customers meet the same need with a different product?Close substitutes available at similar or lower costNo viable substitute; switching requires behavior change
5. Competitive RivalryHow intense is competition among existing players?Many equal competitors, slow growth, commodity product, high exit barriersFew competitors, high growth, differentiated products, rational pricing
Application: Airlines fail almost every Five Forces test (intense rivalry, powerful buyers, commodity product, low switching costs, constant substitutes). Software-as-a-service often passes most of them. The framework tells you why — it's structural, not about individual companies.

Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)

Clayton Christensen's framework: customers don't buy products, they "hire" them to do a job. The milkshake that competes with a bagel for a morning commute isn't competing with other milkshakes — it's competing with breakfast options that are easy to consume while driving.

Key question: What job is the customer trying to get done? What are they really buying this for?

The Value Chain

Porter's value chain breaks a firm into primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing, service) and support activities (infrastructure, HR, tech, procurement). Competitive advantage comes from performing these activities more cheaply or distinctively than rivals.

Unit Economics — The Building Blocks of a Business

Before modeling a whole company, understand the economics of a single customer or transaction.

MetricDefinitionWhy It Matters
CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)Total sales + marketing spend / # new customers acquiredHow expensive is it to get a customer? Rising CAC = competition intensifying or channel saturation.
LTV (Lifetime Value)Average revenue per customer × gross margin × average customer lifespanHow much is a customer worth over their full relationship?
LTV/CAC RatioLTV / CACThe fundamental health metric for a consumer or SaaS business. >3x = healthy. <1x = you lose money on every customer. >5x may mean you're under-investing in growth.
Payback PeriodCAC / (Monthly Revenue per Customer × Gross Margin)Months to recover CAC. <12 months = strong. >24 months = capital-intensive growth.
Churn Rate% of customers (or revenue) lost in a periodThe silent killer. 10% monthly churn = <1 year average customer life. 1% monthly churn = ~100 month life. Compound this.
Gross Margin(Revenue − COGS) / RevenueWhat's left after delivering the product/service before any fixed costs. SaaS: 70–85%. Marketplace: 15–30%. Physical product: 30–60%.
Contribution MarginRevenue − Variable CostsHow much each unit contributes to covering fixed costs. Must be positive for the business to scale.

SaaS Metrics

MetricFormulaBenchmark
ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue)MRR × 12Primary revenue metric for SaaS. Investors pay multiples on ARR.
MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue)Sum of all monthly subscriptions$1M MRR = $12M ARR. Most SaaS targets are in ARR terms.
Net Revenue Retention (NRR)(Starting MRR + expansions − churned − contracted) / Starting MRR>100% = best-in-class (existing customers spend more over time). <90% = churn problem. 120%+ = exceptional (Snowflake, Datadog).
Rule of 40Revenue Growth % + FCF Margin % ≥ 40Balances growth and profitability. A 60% growth company can burn 20% FCF margin. A 10% growth company needs 30%+ margins. Public SaaS companies trade at premiums when >40.
Magic NumberNet New ARR / Previous Quarter S&M Spend>0.75 = efficient growth. <0.5 = sales efficiency problem — spending too much to acquire revenue.

Competitive Strategy — Generic Strategies

Porter identified three sustainable strategies. Companies that try to be all three — or sit between them — often get "stuck in the middle" and earn below-average returns.

StrategyHow It WorksExamplesRisk
Cost LeadershipLowest cost producer in the industry. Can undercut on price or earn more margin at market price.Walmart, Costco, GEICO, Southwest (historically), RyanairTechnology or process innovation can eliminate your cost advantage. Race to the bottom on price.
DifferentiationOffer something buyers value more than competitors — quality, brand, features, service. Command a premium price.Apple, LVMH, Rolex, McKinsey, HermèsPremium erodes if differentiation isn't maintained. Competitors copy features.
Focus (Niche)Serve a specific segment better than anyone else — geographic, demographic, or need-based.Rolls-Royce, Wingstop, Sweetgreen, specialized B2B softwareThe niche may be too small. Larger competitors may decide to enter.

Capital Allocation — What Separates Great CEOs

William Thorndike's The Outsiders argues that the most important skill of a CEO is capital allocation — what to do with the cash the business generates. There are five uses of capital:

Use of CapitalWhen It Makes SenseWhen It Destroys Value
Reinvest in the Business (CapEx, R&D)ROIC > WACC; organic growth opportunities existWhen you're throwing capital at mediocre returns just to grow revenue
Acquisitions (M&A)Priced below intrinsic value; clear synergies; strong integration track recordPaying too much (goodwill impairment risk); acquiring to hide earnings problems; deal-for-deal's-sake
Pay Down DebtHigh leverage, uncertain business model, rising ratesWhen the business is underleveraged and the after-tax cost of debt is cheap
Pay DividendsMature, stable business with no better reinvestment opportunitiesWhen high-return organic opportunities exist; when used to signal confidence rather than genuine cash
Buy Back StockStock is trading below intrinsic value; company has excess cashWhen buying back overvalued stock (destroys value), or using leverage at the peak of the cycle
The Outsiders pattern: Buffett, Singleton (Teledyne), Malone (TCI), Murphy (Capital Cities), Washington (Washington Post) — all allocated capital contrarily: bought back stock aggressively when cheap, avoided acquisitions at peak prices, used leverage opportunistically. They ran decentralized operations and measured success in per-share value, not revenue or empire-building.

Startup Frameworks

The Lean Startup

ConceptWhat It Means
Build-Measure-Learn LoopBuild minimum viable products fast, measure what happens, learn, and iterate. Speed of this loop = competitive advantage for startups.
MVP (Minimum Viable Product)The smallest version of a product that lets you test your core hypothesis with real customers. Not a beta — an experiment.
Pivot vs. PersevereA pivot is a structured course correction — changing one element of a strategy (customer segment, product, channel) while keeping what you've learned.
Vanity Metrics vs. Actionable MetricsVanity: page views, registered users, press mentions. Actionable: activated users, retention cohorts, revenue per customer, NPS.

Y Combinator Principles

  • "Make something people want." Everything else is secondary to this.
  • Talk to users. The biggest mistake early-stage companies make is not talking to actual customers enough.
  • Do things that don't scale. Early on, do things manually, personally. Optimize later.
  • Default alive vs. default dead. Will you reach profitability before you run out of money, without raising more? Know where you stand.
  • Founder-market fit. Are you uniquely positioned to build this? Domain expertise + obsession + insight matters.

Crossing the Chasm

Geoffrey Moore's model: technology adoption follows a curve (Innovators → Early Adopters → Early Majority → Late Majority → Laggards). The "chasm" between Early Adopters and the Early Majority is where most tech startups fail. Early adopters tolerate bugs; the early majority won't. You need to own a specific niche completely before crossing.
