Macro · Micro · Monetary Policy · Key Indicators · Market Structures

Economics is the study of how people allocate scarce resources. Macro looks at the whole economy; micro looks at individual actors and markets.

Macroeconomics — The Big Picture

Core Macro Concepts

ConceptDefinitionWhy It Matters for Investing
GDP (Gross Domestic Product)Total market value of all goods and services produced in a country in a period. GDP = C + I + G + (X−M)The headline measure of economic health. GDP growth = expanding economy. Two consecutive quarters of declining GDP = recession by convention.
InflationGeneral rise in price levels over time. Measured by CPI (Consumer Price Index), PCE, PPI.Erodes purchasing power and bond values. The Fed targets ~2% PCE inflation. High inflation forces rate hikes → compresses stock valuations.
Unemployment Rate% of labor force actively seeking work but unable to find it. Does not count those who stopped looking."Full employment" is ~4–5%. Below that, wage inflation kicks in. Used with inflation in the Fed's dual mandate.
Business CycleRecurring pattern of expansion → peak → contraction → trough → expansion. Typically 5–10 years.Different sectors outperform at different phases: tech/consumer discretionary in expansion; utilities/staples in contraction.
Real vs. NominalNominal = not adjusted for inflation. Real = inflation-adjusted (real GDP, real wages, real interest rates).Always compare real numbers across time. Nominal GDP of $20T vs. $15T is misleading if inflation was 30%.
Current Account / Trade BalanceExports − Imports. Deficit means more goods flow in than out (US runs a chronic deficit).Persistent deficits weaken a currency over time. China's surplus = massive FX reserves and geopolitical leverage.

Monetary Policy — The Fed

The Federal Reserve controls monetary policy. Its dual mandate: maximum employment + stable prices (2% inflation).

ToolHow It WorksEffect
Federal Funds RateThe rate banks charge each other for overnight lending. The Fed sets a target range and influences it through open market operations.Everything else is benchmarked to this: mortgages, corporate bonds, savings accounts. Lower rate = cheaper borrowing = more economic activity.
Open Market Operations (OMO)Fed buys or sells Treasury securities. Buying = injects money (expansionary). Selling = removes money (contractionary).Primary mechanism for hitting the fed funds rate target.
Quantitative Easing (QE)Fed buys long-duration assets (MBS, Treasuries) to suppress long-term rates when short rates are already at zero.Lowers long rates, pushes investors into riskier assets, weakens the dollar. Used in 2008, 2020.
Quantitative Tightening (QT)Opposite of QE — Fed lets bonds mature and doesn't reinvest (or actively sells). Shrinks the balance sheet.Removes liquidity from the system; tightens financial conditions without raising short rates.
Reserve Requirements% of deposits banks must hold in reserve (effectively eliminated in 2020).Raising this constrains lending. Lowering expands credit creation.
Forward GuidanceFed communicates its future intentions to influence expectations now.Markets price in expected rate paths — often moves markets before actual rate changes.

Fiscal Policy — The Government

ToolExpansionary VersionContractionary VersionLag Issue
Government SpendingIncrease spending (infrastructure, transfers)Decrease spending, cut programsInfrastructure bills take years to spend
TaxationCut taxes to boost consumer spendingRaise taxes to reduce demand and cool inflationTax cuts take a cycle to show full impact
Automatic StabilizersUnemployment insurance, progressive taxes — automatically expand/contract without legislation—No lag — kicks in automatically
Fiscal vs. Monetary: Monetary policy is faster (the Fed can change rates at any meeting) but works with a 12–18 month lag. Fiscal policy is slower (requires Congressional action) but can be more targeted. In a crisis, you need both — see 2008 and 2020.

Interest Rates & the Yield Curve

ConceptWhat It IsSignal
Yield CurvePlot of Treasury yields from 3-month to 30-year. Normally upward sloping (longer = higher yield).The most watched macro indicator on Wall Street.
Normal (Steep) CurveLong rates > Short rates. Typically 1.5–2%+ spread on 10yr vs. 2yr.Economy expanding; banks make money borrowing short and lending long.
Flat CurveShort and long rates nearly equal.Economic slowdown expected; late-cycle warning sign.
Inverted CurveShort rates > Long rates (e.g., 2yr yield > 10yr yield).Has preceded every US recession since 1955. The market expects rate cuts ahead → growth slowdown. Not a perfect timing tool but track record is unmatched.
Real Interest RateNominal rate − Inflation. r = i − π.Negative real rates = financial repression. Positive real rates = true tightening. Drives gold, commodities, EM currencies.

Microeconomics — Markets and Behavior

Supply and Demand

LawRelationshipShifts the Curve
Law of DemandPrice ↑ → Quantity Demanded ↓ (inverse)Income, preferences, prices of substitutes/complements, expectations
Law of SupplyPrice ↑ → Quantity Supplied ↑ (direct)Input costs, technology, number of producers, expectations, regulations
EquilibriumWhere supply = demand. Market clears at this price and quantity.Surplus (price above eq.) → price falls. Shortage (price below eq.) → price rises.

Elasticity

TypeFormulaInterpretationExamples
Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)% Δ Quantity / % Δ Price|PED| > 1 = elastic (sensitive to price). |PED| < 1 = inelastic (insensitive).Elastic: luxury goods, vacations. Inelastic: insulin, gasoline, cigarettes.
Income Elasticity% Δ Quantity / % Δ IncomePositive = normal good (buy more as income rises). Negative = inferior good (buy less).Normal: steaks, travel. Inferior: instant ramen, used cars.
Cross-Price Elasticity% Δ Qty of A / % Δ Price of BPositive = substitutes (Pepsi/Coke). Negative = complements (printers/ink).Used in antitrust analysis to define markets

Key Micro Concepts

ConceptWhat It IsApplication
Marginal AnalysisDecision-making based on the next unit. Produce until Marginal Revenue = Marginal Cost.How firms set output levels; why the last customer acquired often drives profitability
Opportunity CostThe value of the next best alternative forgone. The real cost of any decision.Why working at a startup costs more than your salary (you gave up a safer salary + equity elsewhere)
Sunk CostCost already paid and unrecoverable. Irrelevant to future decisions.The sunk cost fallacy — continuing a bad project because you've "already invested so much"
Consumer SurplusDifference between what a consumer would pay and what they actually pay.Platform businesses try to convert consumer surplus into revenue (Amazon Prime pricing, etc.)
Producer SurplusDifference between the price a producer receives and their minimum acceptable price.Gross margin is essentially producer surplus captured per unit
ExternalitiesCosts or benefits imposed on third parties not in a transaction. Negative: pollution. Positive: education, vaccines.Market failure — leads to over/under production. Governments tax negative externalities (carbon) and subsidize positive ones (education).
Moral HazardTaking more risk because you're protected from consequences.Banks taking excessive risks knowing they'll be bailed out; insurance encouraging risky behavior
Adverse SelectionHigher-risk people/products disproportionately seek insurance or credit (information asymmetry).Why insurance markets fail without mandates; why lenders require credit scores
Game TheoryStudy of strategic decisions where outcomes depend on others' actions. Nash Equilibrium = no player wants to change strategy unilaterally.Pricing wars, OPEC output decisions, trade negotiations, poker
Price DiscriminationCharging different prices to different customers for the same product.1st degree (custom pricing), 2nd degree (bulk discounts), 3rd degree (student prices, geographic pricing)

Key Economic Indicators

IndicatorWhat It MeasuresLeading / LaggingWhere to Find
CPI (Consumer Price Index)Price changes for a basket of consumer goods. Main inflation gauge.LaggingBLS (bls.gov) monthly
PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures)Fed's preferred inflation gauge. Broader than CPI; weights adjust with behavior.LaggingBEA (bea.gov) monthly
PPI (Producer Price Index)Prices at the wholesale/producer level. Leads CPI — if input costs rise, consumer prices follow.Leading (for CPI)BLS monthly
Nonfarm Payrolls (NFP)Monthly change in employed workers outside farms. The most market-moving economic release.LaggingBLS, first Friday of each month
ISM Manufacturing / Services PMISurvey of purchasing managers. Above 50 = expansion; below 50 = contraction.LeadingISM, first week of each month
Retail SalesMonthly change in consumer spending on goods. 2/3 of US economy is consumer spending.CoincidentCensus Bureau monthly
Initial Jobless ClaimsWeekly new unemployment filings. Spikes = labor market weakening.LeadingDOL every Thursday
Housing Starts / PermitsNew residential construction. Highly cyclical; large multiplier effects.LeadingCensus Bureau monthly
Conference Board LEIComposite of 10 leading indicators. Sustained decline signals recession within 6–12 months.LeadingConference Board monthly
Federal Funds FuturesMarket-implied probability of rate changes at future Fed meetings.Leading (market expectation)CME FedWatch Tool

Big Economic Schools of Thought

SchoolCore BeliefPolicy PrescriptionKey Thinkers
KeynesianDemand drives the economy. In recessions, private demand falls short — government must fill the gap.Fiscal stimulus, deficit spending in downturns; government as "spender of last resort"Keynes, Krugman, Stiglitz
MonetaristMoney supply drives inflation and output. Central bank control of money supply is paramount.Stable, predictable money growth; Fed should follow rules not discretionMilton Friedman
AustrianFree markets are self-correcting. Government intervention creates distortions and booms/busts.Minimal intervention, sound money, let recessions clear bad investmentsHayek, Mises
Supply-SideGrowth comes from increasing production capacity through incentives.Cut taxes on capital and businesses, deregulate; let supply expand to meet demandLaffer, Reagan-era economists
Modern Monetary Theory (MMT)Governments that issue their own currency can't go broke; deficits are just net financial assets for the private sector.Spend freely until inflation forces spending reduction; taxes don't fund spending — they remove money from the economyKelton, Mosler
