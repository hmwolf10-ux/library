Philosophy is literally "love of wisdom" — asking the most fundamental questions about existence, knowledge, morality, and how to live. It's not abstract nonsense. It's the operating system for everything else.

The Major Schools

Stoicism — The Most Practically Useful Philosophy

"You have power over your mind, not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength." — Marcus Aurelius

Founded by Zeno of Citium (~300 BCE). Reached its peak with three Romans: Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius.

Core IdeaWhat It MeansApplication
The Dichotomy of ControlSome things are "up to us" (our judgments, desires, actions). Everything else is not (weather, others' behavior, death, reputation). Focus only on what's up to you.The most powerful mindset shift in philosophy. Anxiety comes from trying to control what you can't. Stoicism cuts that loop.
Virtue Is the Only GoodExternal things (wealth, health, status) are "preferred indifferents" — nice to have but not essential to a good life. Only virtue (wisdom, courage, justice, temperance) is genuinely good.Not nihilism — you should still pursue good outcomes. But your happiness and worth don't depend on achieving them.
Live According to NatureReason is our defining characteristic as humans. Living well means living rationally and virtuously — not following impulse, fashion, or fear.The Stoic questions: Is this reaction rational? Is this choice in line with my values?
Negative Visualization (Premeditatio Malorum)Deliberately imagine losing what you value — your health, relationships, possessions. Not to be morbid, but to appreciate what you have and prepare for adversity without panic.Marcus Aurelius imagined his children's deaths; Seneca meditated on his own. Not pessimism — pre-emptive gratitude and resilience.
Memento Mori"Remember you will die." Used not to depress but to clarify — if this is your last day, are you spending it on what matters?Every Stoic emperor and senator kept a skull on their desk. Not morbid — clarifying.

Key texts: Meditations (Marcus Aurelius — free on Project Gutenberg), Letters from a Stoic (Seneca), Enchiridion (Epictetus).

Existentialism — The 20th Century Response to a Godless World

"Existence precedes essence." — Jean-Paul Sartre

Emerged primarily in post-WWI and post-WWII Europe, as traditional religion and Enlightenment optimism both collapsed. Asks: if there's no God, no fixed human nature, no inherent meaning — what then?

ThinkerCore IdeaKey Work
Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855)Proto-existentialist. Existence is subjective — you can't find truth through reason alone; you must choose and commit. The "leap of faith." Three stages of life: aesthetic, ethical, religious.Either/Or; Fear and Trembling
Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900)"God is dead" — meaning Western civilization's moral framework has collapsed. Must create new values. Will to Power — not domination, but self-overcoming. The Übermensch: one who creates their own values.Thus Spoke Zarathustra; Beyond Good and Evil; The Gay Science
Martin Heidegger (1889–1976)Being-in-the-world — we don't experience the world as detached observers but as participants. Authenticity vs. "das Man" (the They) — living your own life vs. conforming to what "one" does.Being and Time
Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980)Radical freedom — we are "condemned to be free." No fixed essence precedes our existence; we define ourselves through choices. Bad faith = pretending you have no choice when you do.Being and Nothingness; No Exit; Existentialism is a Humanism
Albert Camus (1913–1960)The Absurd — life has no inherent meaning but we desperately seek it. Three responses: suicide (rejection), leap of faith (Kierkegaard's escape), or rebellion (accept the absurd and live fully anyway). "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."The Myth of Sisyphus; The Stranger; The Plague
Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986)Applied existentialism to gender — "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." Freedom and oppression as existential categories.The Second Sex; The Ethics of Ambiguity

The Ancient Greeks — The Foundation

PhilosopherDatesCore IdeasWhy He Matters
Socrates470–399 BCEThe Socratic method — ask questions until contradictions surface. "I know that I know nothing." Philosophy as a way of life, not just an academic subject. Died rather than compromise his principles.The model of intellectual integrity. He wrote nothing — everything is through Plato.
Plato428–348 BCETheory of Forms: the physical world is a shadow of abstract ideal Forms (the perfect Chair, Justice, Beauty). The philosopher's job is to ascend from appearances to reality. Philosopher-kings should rule.Whitehead called all Western philosophy "footnotes to Plato." The Republic is still the most important political philosophy text.
Aristotle384–322 BCERejected Plato's Forms — knowledge comes from observation, not pure reason. Ethics: eudaimonia (flourishing/happiness) is the highest good, achieved through virtue and reason. Everything in moderation (the Golden Mean).Invented formal logic, biology, physics, political science, literary criticism. Possibly the most brilliant mind in history.
Epicurus341–270 BCEThe goal is ataraxia — tranquility, not pleasure-seeking (despite common misconception). Pleasure = absence of pain and anxiety. Friends, simple food, philosophical community over wealth and ambition. Death is nothing — you won't experience it.Often mischaracterized as hedonism. Actually closer to Stoicism — pursue peace, not sensation.

The Rationalists vs. Empiricists

The defining battle of 17th–18th century philosophy: where does knowledge come from?

CampClaimKey Figures
RationalistsReason alone can give us genuine knowledge. Some truths are innate — known a priori without experience. Math is the model: we can know 2+2=4 without observing the world.Descartes ("I think therefore I am"), Spinoza, Leibniz
EmpiricistsAll knowledge comes from sensory experience. The mind is a blank slate at birth (tabula rasa). Nothing is known without first being perceived.Locke, Berkeley, Hume
Kant's SynthesisBoth are right — but incomplete. The mind actively structures experience. Space, time, and causality are categories the mind imposes on experience, not things discovered in it. "Concepts without percepts are empty; percepts without concepts are blind."Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

Ethics — The Three Main Frameworks

Every ethical question in business, politics, and life runs through one of these three frameworks. Knowing all three makes you a better thinker.

FrameworkCore QuestionWhat MattersKey ThinkersStrengthsWeaknesses
Consequentialism (Utilitarianism)What produces the best outcomes?Results. An action is right if it maximizes well-being (pleasure, happiness, flourishing) for the greatest number.Bentham, Mill, Peter SingerIntuitive, flexible, results-oriented. Forces you to think about actual impact.Can justify atrocities if the math works out (torture one person to save five?). Ignores rights, justice, character.
DeontologyWhat is my duty?Rules and duties. Some actions are right or wrong in themselves, regardless of consequences. Never use people as mere means.Kant (Categorical Imperative: act only on maxims you could universalize)Protects individual rights. Provides clear rules. Can't be manipulated by "the ends justify the means."Rigid — what if following the rule produces disaster? What when duties conflict?
Virtue EthicsWhat kind of person should I be?Character. Focus on cultivating virtues (courage, honesty, justice, prudence) rather than following rules or calculating outcomes.Aristotle, MacIntyre, Confucius (parallel tradition)Focuses on who you are, not just what you do. Wisdom and judgment over rule-following. More realistic about moral psychology.Vague on what to do in specific cases. Which virtues? Whose virtues?
In practice: No one framework is complete. Good moral reasoning draws on all three. Consequentialist thinking for policy (what produces the best outcomes?). Deontological thinking for individual rights (can I do this to this person?). Virtue ethics for character formation (what kind of person am I becoming?). The Stoics were primarily virtue ethicists; Utilitarianism dominates modern economics and policy; Kant is the foundation of human rights law.

The Big Questions — The Live Debates

Free Will vs. Determinism

PositionClaimImplication
Hard DeterminismAll events including human choices are fully determined by prior causes (physics, genes, environment). Free will is an illusion.No one is truly morally responsible for anything. Punishment is unjustified; only rehabilitation and deterrence matter.
Libertarian Free WillHumans have genuine free will that isn't determined by prior causes. We are the ultimate source of our choices.Full moral responsibility. Compatible with most religious views.
CompatibilismFree will and determinism are compatible. "Free" means uncoerced by external force — not uncaused. The dominant position among philosophers.You acted freely when you weren't forced, even if your brain was determined to act. Responsibility is meaningful within this framework.

The Hard Problem of Consciousness

Philosopher David Chalmers named this: we can explain all the brain's information-processing functions (the "easy problems"). What we can't explain is why any of it produces subjective experience — why there is something it is like to be you. Why doesn't all that neural activity happen in the dark, without any inner experience?

Positions: Physicalism (consciousness is just brain activity, no mystery — most scientists). Dualism (mind and matter are distinct substances — Descartes). Panpsychism (consciousness is fundamental in nature, like mass or charge). Illusionism (the "hard problem" is itself an illusion).

This is still genuinely unsolved. Some of the sharpest minds in philosophy and neuroscience disagree about even what kind of problem this is.

The Existence of God

ArgumentWhat It ClaimsKey Objection
Cosmological Argument (Aquinas)Everything that exists has a cause. The universe exists. Therefore it has a cause — God (the uncaused first cause).Why can't the universe itself be the uncaused first cause? Special pleading for God.
Teleological / Design ArgumentThe universe shows signs of design (fine-tuning of physical constants, complexity of life). Design implies a designer.Darwin's natural selection explains complexity without design. The universe looks designed because we're here to observe it (anthropic principle).
Ontological Argument (Anselm)God is defined as the greatest conceivable being. A being that exists in reality is greater than one that exists only in thought. Therefore God must exist in reality.You can't define things into existence. I can define a perfect island — it doesn't make it real.
Problem of EvilArgument against God: if an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good God exists, why does evil and suffering exist?Theist responses: free will defense; soul-making theodicy; God's ways are beyond our understanding.

Stoicism in Practice — The Exercises

Stoicism was always more practical than academic. Here are the actual exercises they used:

  • Morning review: What might go wrong today? What obstacles might I face? How will I respond with virtue? (Premeditatio malorum — premeditate on the bad)
  • Evening review: What did I do well today? What could I have done better? Where did I fail to act in accordance with my values? (Seneca did this nightly)
  • The View from Above: Mentally zoom out — picture your city, country, planet from space. Your problem is tiny. This disrupts the tunnel vision of stress and ego.
  • Voluntary discomfort: Periodically choose discomfort — skip a meal, sleep on the floor, take a cold shower. Trains you to realize you can handle more than you think, and reduces fear of hardship.
  • Amor Fati: Love your fate — not just accept it, but embrace everything that happens as necessary and good. Nietzsche borrowed this directly from the Stoics.
  • The Inner Citadel: Your mind is a fortress that can't be conquered without your permission. External events can harm your body and reputation — but not your character or your judgment unless you let them.
