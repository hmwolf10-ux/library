Financial Ratios

Valuation, profitability, liquidity, solvency, and efficiency — every ratio you'll use in practice.

Valuation Ratios — Is the stock cheap or expensive?

These ratios only tell you price relative to a metric — always compare to (1) the company's own history, (2) industry peers, and (3) the broader market. Context is everything.
RatioFormulaWhat It MeasuresBenchmark / Signal
P/E RatioPrice / EPSHow much investors pay per $1 of earnings. The most cited valuation metric.10–20x = reasonable; >30x = growth priced in; <10x = cheap or troubled
Forward P/EPrice / Next-Year EPS EstimateSame as P/E but uses projected earnings — more forward-looking.Compare vs. historical average and sector peers
PEG RatioP/E / EPS Growth RateAdjusts P/E for growth — a P/E of 30 with 30% growth is a PEG of 1.0, which is fair.<1.0 = potentially undervalued; >2.0 = expensive relative to growth
EV/EBITDAEnterprise Value / EBITDACapital-structure-neutral valuation. Better than P/E for comparing companies with different debt levels.6–12x = typical; used heavily in M&A and LBO analysis
EV/RevenueEnterprise Value / RevenueUsed for companies with no earnings yet (startups, high-growth SaaS).1–3x = mature; 5–15x = high-growth; varies massively by sector
P/B RatioPrice / Book Value per ShareCompares market price to net asset value. Classic value investing metric.<1.0 = trading below assets (often a value flag); >3x = intangible-heavy
P/S RatioPrice / Revenue per SharePrice relative to sales — useful when earnings are negative.1–3x = reasonable for mature businesses; varies by margin profile
Dividend YieldAnnual Dividend / Stock PriceCash return to shareholders as a % of price.>4% = high yield (check if sustainable); 1–3% = typical for stable companies

Profitability Ratios — How well does the business make money?

RatioFormulaWhat It MeasuresBenchmark / Signal
Gross MarginGross Profit / RevenueProfit after cost of goods sold. Shows pricing power and production efficiency.>50% = strong (SaaS, pharma); 20–40% = retail/manufacturing typical
Operating MarginOperating Income / RevenueProfit after operating expenses. Shows how well the core business runs.>20% = excellent; 10–20% = solid; <5% = thin — watch for swings
Net Profit MarginNet Income / RevenueThe bottom line as a % of revenue. What's actually left after everything.Varies widely by industry — compare within sector
EBITDA MarginEBITDA / RevenueOperating profitability before non-cash and financing items. Most used in valuation.>30% = very strong; 15–25% = healthy; <10% = watch closely
ROENet Income / Shareholders' EquityReturn on equity — how efficiently the company generates profit from shareholders' money.>15% = good; >20% = excellent; Buffett looks for consistent >15%
ROANet Income / Total AssetsHow efficiently assets generate profit. Better cross-industry comparison than ROE.>5% = solid; >10% = excellent; banks typically run 1–2%
ROICNOPAT / Invested CapitalThe gold standard return metric — how well the business deploys capital into the actual business.>10% = good; >15% = exceptional; compare to WACC — if ROIC > WACC, value is being created
ROCEEBIT / Capital EmployedLike ROIC but uses EBIT. Common in European analysis and capital-intensive industries.>15% = strong; compare to cost of capital

Liquidity & Solvency Ratios — Can it survive and pay its debts?

Solvency ratios matter most in downturns. A company can be profitable and still go bankrupt if it can't service its debt. Always stress-test these.
RatioFormulaWhat It MeasuresBenchmark / Signal
Current RatioCurrent Assets / Current LiabilitiesCan the company pay short-term bills with short-term assets?>2.0 = very comfortable; 1.0–2.0 = fine; <1.0 = potential liquidity risk
Quick Ratio(Cash + AR) / Current LiabilitiesStricter version — excludes inventory (which may not be quickly converted to cash).>1.0 = healthy; <0.5 = watch closely
Cash RatioCash / Current LiabilitiesMost conservative — only counts actual cash. Can the company pay bills with cash on hand?<0.5 is common and fine; context-dependent
Debt/EquityTotal Debt / Shareholders' EquityHow leveraged is the company? High D/E means more financial risk.<1.0 = conservative; 1–2x = moderate; >2x = highly leveraged
Debt/EBITDATotal Debt / EBITDAHow many years of earnings would it take to pay off all debt? Standard leverage metric in lending.<2x = low; 2–4x = moderate; >4x = high; >6x = distressed territory
Interest CoverageEBIT / Interest ExpenseCan the company comfortably cover its interest payments?>3x = safe; <1.5x = concerning; <1x = the company can't cover interest from operations
Net DebtTotal Debt – CashThe real debt burden after netting out cash the company holds.Negative net debt = net cash position (fortress balance sheet)

Efficiency Ratios — How well does it use its assets?

RatioFormulaWhat It MeasuresBenchmark / Signal
Asset TurnoverRevenue / Total AssetsHow efficiently assets generate revenue. Higher = leaner, more efficient business.Varies by industry — retail ~2x, utilities ~0.3x
Inventory TurnoverCOGS / Average InventoryHow fast inventory sells. High = efficient; low = slow-moving or overstocked.Higher is better; compare to industry peers
Days Sales OutstandingAR / (Revenue / 365)Average days to collect payment from customers. Lower = getting paid faster.<30 days = excellent; >60 days = watch for collection issues
Days Payable OutstandingAP / (COGS / 365)Average days to pay suppliers. Higher can mean better cash management (or strained relationships).30–60 days typical; very high may signal cash stress
Cash Conversion CycleDIO + DSO – DPOHow long cash is tied up in operations. Negative = company collects before it pays (Amazon model).Negative or near-zero = excellent cash efficiency
FCF MarginFree Cash Flow / RevenueWhat % of revenue becomes actual cash. The most important margin metric for investors.>15% = very strong; compare to net margin — gap shows working capital efficiency
FCF YieldFCF per Share / Stock PriceThe cash equivalent of earnings yield. How much FCF you get per dollar invested.>5% = attractive; compare to bond yields for context

Financial Statements

Every line item explained — what it is, where it comes from, and what to watch for.

The three statements are connected: Net Income flows from the Income Statement into Retained Earnings on the Balance Sheet and is the starting point of the Cash Flow Statement. Changes in Balance Sheet accounts drive working capital movements in the CFO section.

Income Statement — The Profit Story

Line ItemWhat It IsWhat to Watch
Revenue (Net Sales)Total income from selling goods/services after returns and discounts. The top line.Growth rate YoY, organic vs. acquired growth, guidance vs. actual
Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)Direct costs of producing what was sold — materials, direct labor, manufacturing overhead.Rising COGS as % of revenue = margin compression. Watch vs. revenue growth
Gross ProfitRevenue minus COGS. The foundation of all downstream profitability.Gross margin % trend — expanding = pricing power or efficiency gains
Operating ExpensesSG&A (selling, general & admin) + R&D + other ongoing costs of running the business.Is opex growing faster or slower than revenue? Slower = operating leverage
EBIT / Operating IncomeGross Profit minus OpEx. Profit from core operations before interest and taxes.Operating margin — the best indicator of core business health
Interest ExpenseCost of servicing debt — paid to bondholders and lenders.Large vs. EBIT = financial risk. Rising = company is borrowing more or rates are up
EBT (Pre-Tax Income)EBIT minus interest expense (and plus any interest income).Effective tax rate often explained here — watch for one-time items
Income Tax ExpenseTax owed on pretax income. Effective rate often differs from statutory rate due to deductions.Large gap between statutory and effective rate = dig into what's driving it
Net IncomeThe bottom line. Profit available to common shareholders after all expenses and taxes.EPS growth, quality of earnings (is it cash or accrual-heavy?)
EPS (Basic)Net Income / Basic Shares Outstanding. Earnings per share before dilution.Always compare diluted EPS — options and convertibles can matter a lot
EPS (Diluted)Net Income / Fully Diluted Share Count. Includes options, warrants, convertibles.If diluted EPS is much lower than basic, heavy dilution is occurring — bad for shareholders
EBITDAEBIT + Depreciation + Amortization. A proxy for operating cash flow. Not a GAAP figure.Most used in valuation multiples — but doesn't account for capex, so not true cash flow

Balance Sheet — Assets

Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Always. If your balance sheet doesn't balance, something is wrong.
Line ItemWhat It IsWhat to Watch
Cash & EquivalentsActual cash plus Treasury bills and money market funds. The most liquid asset.Large cash position = financial safety, buyback potential, or inability to deploy capital
Accounts ReceivableMoney owed to the company by customers for goods/services delivered but not yet paid.Rising faster than revenue = customers paying slower (bad); check allowance for doubtful accounts
InventoryGoods held for sale or raw materials. Valued at cost (FIFO or LIFO).Rising inventory with flat sales = demand problem; write-downs signal obsolescence
Prepaid ExpensesExpenses paid in advance (insurance, subscriptions) — they'll be expensed as time passes.Usually immaterial — flag if unusually large vs. revenue
PP&E (Net)Property, Plant & Equipment minus accumulated depreciation. The core fixed assets.Capex intensity = capex / revenue. High capex businesses have less FCF
GoodwillPremium paid above fair value in acquisitions. Only appears after M&A. Not amortized under GAAP.Large goodwill = acquisitive company. Impairment charges wipe it off the income statement
Intangible AssetsBrands, patents, customer relationships, software — identified in acquisitions.Amortized over time — reduces earnings but not cash. Watch for impairments
Total AssetsEverything the company owns or controls. Must equal Total Liabilities + Equity.Assets = Liabilities + Equity is the foundational accounting equation

Balance Sheet — Liabilities & Equity

Line ItemWhat It IsWhat to Watch
Accounts PayableMoney the company owes to suppliers for goods/services received but not yet paid.Rising AP vs. COGS = company is taking longer to pay — can be good (cash management) or bad (stress)
Accrued LiabilitiesExpenses incurred but not yet paid in cash — wages, taxes, utilities.Large accruals vs. operating expenses = timing differences to watch
Deferred RevenueCash received from customers before the service/product is delivered. A liability until earned.Common in SaaS — growing deferred revenue = strong future revenue pipeline
Short-Term DebtDebt due within 12 months — commercial paper, current portion of long-term debt.Large short-term debt with low cash = refinancing risk
Long-Term DebtBonds, term loans, and other debt due beyond 12 months.Total debt / EBITDA is the key leverage ratio. Compare to peers and covenants
Shareholders' EquityAssets minus Liabilities. The book value belonging to shareholders.Declining equity over time = losses or heavy buybacks. Negative equity is not always a red flag (buybacks)
Retained EarningsCumulative net income kept in the business rather than paid as dividends.Rising retained earnings = profitable and reinvesting. Declining = losses or big dividends/buybacks

Cash Flow Statement — The Cash Reality

Net income is an opinion. Cash flow is a fact. The CFO is the most important statement for assessing business quality — it's harder to fake than earnings.
Line ItemWhat It IsWhat to Watch
Net Income (starting point)CFO starts with net income and adjusts for non-cash items to get to actual cash.The bridge between income statement and cash reality — always start here
D&A Add-BackDepreciation and amortization are non-cash charges — added back since no cash left.Large D&A vs. capex = company spending less than it depreciates (aging assets or asset-light)
Working Capital ChangesChanges in AR, inventory, AP, and accruals — these consume or release cash.Rising AR/inventory = cash consumed. Rising AP = cash conserved. Watch the trend
Cash from Operations (CFO)Total cash generated by core business operations. The most important cash line.CFO should exceed net income over time. Persistent CFO < net income = earnings quality problem
Capital ExpendituresCash spent on PP&E — maintenance capex keeps assets running; growth capex expands them.Maintenance vs. growth capex split matters. Buffett focuses on owner earnings (CFO – maintenance capex)
Free Cash FlowCFO minus Capex. The real cash the business generates that can be distributed or reinvested.The #1 metric for intrinsic valuation. Growing FCF over time = compounding machine
AcquisitionsCash paid for buying other companies — appears in investing activities.Serial acquirers: watch goodwill buildup and whether acquisitions actually create value
Cash from FinancingDebt issuance/repayment, equity issuance, dividends, and share buybacks.Buybacks + dividends = capital returned to shareholders. Constant equity issuance = dilution
Net Change in CashCFO + CFI + CFF. The actual increase or decrease in the cash balance.Should roughly tie to the change in cash on the balance sheet — if it doesn't, dig in

Debits & Credits

Every common journal entry — the backbone of accounting. Green = Debit, Red = Credit.

The Golden Rule: Debits = Credits. Every single transaction has at least one debit and one credit of equal value. Assets and Expenses increase with Debits. Liabilities, Equity, and Revenue increase with Credits. DEALER: Dividends, Expenses, Assets → Debit to increase. Liabilities, Equity, Revenue → Credit to increase.
