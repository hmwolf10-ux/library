1) What Is a Portfolio?

A portfolio is the sum of all your investments, across all your accounts (e.g., Roth IRA + brokerage + later a 401(k)), including stocks, ETFs, mutual funds, bonds, and cash. Think of it as your whole investing picture, not just one account at a time.

Your portfolio’s job is to grow your money over time by owning assets that can increase in value or pay income. It’s different from a checking account because the value fluctuates daily as markets move, which is normal and expected.

A key principle is diversification—holding many companies and asset types so that when some are down, others may be up. This helps smooth the ride and reduces the risk of a single mistake hurting your total wealth.

On Fidelity, the Portfolio Summary view shows your total value across accounts plus a breakdown by account. From here you can drill into each account, see what you own (Positions), how much cash is available, and how your investments are performing over various time frames.

2) Brokerage Account (Taxable)

What it is

A brokerage account is your general-purpose investing account. You can deposit money at any time and invest in stocks, ETFs, mutual funds, and bonds. It’s called taxable because you may owe taxes on dividends/interest each year and on gains when you sell for more than you paid.

Key features

No contribution limits: Add as much as you want, whenever you want. This makes the brokerage account ideal for flexible goals beyond retirement (house, car, travel, or just building wealth).

No withdrawal restrictions: You can sell and withdraw at any time. There are no penalties for accessing your money, though selling may create a taxable event (a gain or loss you report at tax time).

Tax notes

Dividends/interest are generally taxable in the year received.

Capital gains are taxable when you sell investments for a profit; holding more than a year can qualify for lower long-term rates.

Capital losses can potentially offset gains, reducing taxes—one useful feature of a taxable account.

On Fidelity

You’ll see Cash Available to Trade/Withdraw, your Positions (with columns like Quantity, Last Price, Market Value, Day Change, Total Gain/Loss, and often Cost Basis), and your Activity/History (deposits, trades, dividends).

This is where you can move money in from your bank (as a deposit), buy investments, and sell when you need funds for shorter-term goals.

3) Roth IRA (Retirement)

What it is

A Roth IRA is a retirement account where you contribute after-tax money and your investments can grow tax-free. If you follow the rules, withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, which is incredibly powerful over decades of compounding.

Important rules/features

Retirement focus: The Roth is designed to stay invested for the long term. You can always withdraw your contributions (the dollars you put in) without tax or penalty, but withdrawing earnings early usually triggers tax plus a 10% penalty (unless you qualify for exceptions).

Annual contribution limit: You can only contribute up to the IRS limit each year (must have earned income). Even modest monthly contributions add up and benefit from tax-free growth over time.

Income eligibility: Very high incomes reduce or block contributions (not typically an issue at age 20, but good to know for the future).

No RMDs during your lifetime: You aren’t forced to withdraw money at a certain age, which gives you maximum flexibility in retirement planning.

On Fidelity

You’ll see your Roth IRA as a separate account with Positions, Performance, and often a Contribution Tracker (showing how much you’ve contributed this tax year).

The trading experience is the same as a brokerage account—just make sure the Roth IRA account is selected when you place orders so the investments live inside the Roth’s tax-free “wrapper.”

4) Brokerage vs. Roth IRA — Key Differences

Taxes on growth

Roth IRA: Earnings aren’t taxed while inside the account; growth is tax-free if you follow withdrawal rules in retirement.

Brokerage: Earnings are taxed as you go (dividends/interest each year, gains when you sell).

Taxes on withdrawals

Roth IRA: Qualified retirement withdrawals (59½+ and 5-year rule) are tax-free; that includes growth.

Brokerage: Withdrawals are penalty-free anytime; you just handle taxes at the time of sales and on income received along the way.

Access & penalties

Roth IRA: Earnings taken out before 59½ typically face tax + 10% penalty (with exceptions). Contributions are accessible anytime.

Brokerage: No penalties or timing restrictions—use money whenever you need it.

Limits & eligibility

Roth IRA: Annual cap on contributions; must have earned income; very high incomes face contribution limits.

Brokerage: No cap, no income rules.

Intended use

Roth IRA: Retirement—maximize long-term compounding with tax-free withdrawals later.

Brokerage: Flexible goals—near-term, medium-term, and extra long-term investing beyond the Roth limit.

Other features

Brokerage: May offer advanced tools (margin/shorts if approved)—not necessary for beginners.

Roth IRA: The unique value is the tax-free growth and tax-free retirement withdrawals.

Bottom line

Use the Roth IRA for retirement (tax-free power over decades).

Use the brokerage for flexibility and goals before retirement.

Many people do both: try to max the Roth each year, then invest extra in the brokerage.

5) How Both Accounts Work Together

Roth IRA = retirement engine: Money here should be viewed as your future-self fund—invest it and let it compound for decades without worrying about yearly taxes on growth.

Brokerage = flexible growth: Invest for shorter or uncertain timelines, and for amounts beyond the Roth limit. You control when to add money and when to take it out, which is great for evolving life goals.

Emergency fund in bank: Keep 3–6 months of expenses in a savings account so you don’t have to sell investments at a bad time or touch your Roth contributions for surprise expenses.

Funding flow: Link your bank account to Fidelity. Then transfer dollars into either your Roth IRA (counts toward the annual limit) or your brokerage (no limit). Double-check the destination account before you submit the transfer.

Viewing the big picture: Fidelity’s Portfolio view shows your combined total and lets you see the allocation across accounts. You can compare how much sits in the Roth vs. the brokerage and watch both grow over time.

6) Navigating Fidelity (Viewing Accounts/Portfolio)

Where to look

Start at Accounts & Trade > Portfolio (web) or the Accounts tab (app). You’ll see a list of accounts with balances (e.g., “Individual” for the brokerage and “Roth IRA”). The top line often shows your total portfolio value across all accounts.

Selecting and understanding an account

Click an account name to open its dashboard. Key areas:

Balances:

Cash Available to Trade (uninvested cash you can use to buy).

Cash Available to Withdraw (cash you can move back to your bank; note that recent sales may take time to fully settle).

Positions: A table of everything you own with columns such as:

Symbol/Name (e.g., AAPL)

Quantity/Shares (how much you own)

Last Price (latest market price per share)

Market Value (Quantity × Price)

Day Change (how much value moved today)

Total Gain/Loss (overall change since purchase)

Cost Basis (what you originally paid; helpful for understanding gains/losses)

Activity/History: Deposits, withdrawals, trades, dividends, interest—use this to audit what’s happened in the account.

Performance views

Use the Performance or Analysis sections to see charts of your account value over chosen time frames (e.g., 1M/3M/YTD/1Y/Max), plus your rate of return. You can also view your asset allocation (e.g., % in U.S. stocks vs. international vs. bonds vs. cash). This helps you visualize diversification and track progress rather than focusing on daily noise.

Mobile vs. web

The mobile app mirrors much of the web functionality in a compact layout. You can quickly check balances, positions, day change, and place trades. For deeper performance analysis and more screen real estate, the web experience is typically easier for learning and review.

Common Fidelity tips

Confirm you’re in the correct account (Roth vs. Brokerage) before placing trades or moving money.

In account settings, you can choose to reinvest dividends (DRIP) or have them paid to cash—DRIP makes compounding automatic.

For Roth contributions, check the “Tax Year” label when funding late in the year or early the next year so your contribution counts toward the intended year.

7) Placing Trades on Fidelity (Buy/Sell)

Buy a stock/ETF (step-by-step)

Navigate: Go to Accounts & Trade > Trade (web) or tap Trade in the app.

Select account: Choose Roth IRA or Brokerage from the dropdown. This determines where the investment will live, so double-check.

Enter symbol: Type the ticker (e.g., AAPL for Apple). If unsure, you can search by company/fund name.

Choose action: Select Buy. (Use Sell when exiting or trimming a position.)

Set amount: Enter shares (e.g., 5) or a dollar amount (e.g., $200). Fidelity supports fractional shares for many stocks/ETFs, so you can invest exact dollar amounts.

Order type:

Market order buys at the current best available price and typically fills immediately during market hours. Simple and fast.

Limit order sets a maximum price you’re willing to pay (buys only if the market hits that price or lower). Offers price control but might not fill.

Time-in-force: Day (expires end of trading day) or Good ’til Canceled (stays open until filled or you cancel, typically up to a set period).

Preview: Review the account, symbol, quantity/amount, order type, estimated cost, and any notes. This is your chance to catch mistakes before submitting.

Submit: Place the order. A confirmation appears with an order number. Market orders usually execute within seconds during market hours; limit orders show as Open/Pending until filled or canceled.

After the trade

The position appears under Positions with cost basis and real-time gain/loss.

Check Activity & Orders to see filled orders and to cancel any still-open limit orders.

Selling

Use the same ticket: select Sell, choose shares or dollar amount, and pick Market or Limit.

Brokerage: Sales may create realized gains/losses (reportable at tax time).

Roth IRA: No tax when you sell inside the account; taxes only matter if you withdraw earnings early from the Roth.

Extra trading tips

Avoid placing market orders in pre-/post-market unless you understand the risks (wider spreads, lower liquidity).

If investing long-term, it’s fine to place a simple market order during normal hours; the tiny price differences rarely matter over years.

8) Fundamental Portfolio Tips

Think long-term: Markets move daily; volatility is normal. By focusing on your multi-year path, you avoid emotional decisions. Your Roth is especially about decades of compounding—small contributions now can become large sums later.

Diversify: Owning broad market index funds/ETFs is an effective way to spread risk across hundreds or thousands of companies with a single purchase, often at low cost. Diversification helps you stay invested through rough patches.

Contribute regularly: Pick a monthly amount and automate it if possible. In the Roth, aim to reach the annual limit if you can. In the brokerage, continue putting extra savings to work on a consistent schedule.

Prioritize the Roth: Given the tax-free retirement withdrawals, it’s generally smart to max the Roth first each year, then invest additional savings in the brokerage for flexibility.

Match risk to timeline: For near-term goals, consider more conservative choices in the brokerage. For retirement (Roth), you can afford more growth-oriented investments due to your long time horizon.

Maintain an emergency fund: Keep cash in a bank savings account for surprises. This reduces the risk of selling investments at the wrong time or dipping into your Roth contributions.

Know the tax basics (brokerage): Expect a 1099 at tax time for dividends/interest and sales. Understand short- vs. long-term gains and keep records (Fidelity tracks cost basis for you).

Keep learning: Fidelity’s beginner content, webinars, and help articles can answer questions as you evolve. Gradually build knowledge; you don’t need advanced strategies to be successful—time in the market and consistent contributions do most of the heavy lifting.

9) Final Thoughts (Recap)

Brokerage = flexible, no limits, taxable—best for any goal and for investing beyond Roth limits; easy access if you need the money.

Roth IRA = after-tax in, tax-free growth, tax-free retirement out—best for retirement; incredibly powerful at age 20 due to decades of compounding.

Using both together is common: max the Roth when you can, then invest extra in the brokerage.

On Fidelity, it’s straightforward to view both accounts, transfer from your bank, buy/sell investments, and track performance.

Starting early puts you in a fantastic position—even small monthly steps can lead to big outcomes over time. Stay consistent, stay diversified, and keep your eye on the long term. 🎉📈
