The Financial Order of Operations

When you get a dollar, there's an optimal order for deploying it. Do step 1 before step 2, step 2 before step 3. This is the sequence that maximizes your long-term wealth.

StepWhat to DoWhy FirstTarget
1. Employer MatchContribute enough to your 401k to get the full employer matchEmployer match is a 50–100% instant return on your money. Nothing beats it. Not doing this is leaving free money on the table.Whatever % gets you the full match (typically 3–6% of salary)
2. High-Interest DebtPay off any debt with interest rate above ~6–7%Paying off 20% APR credit card debt is a guaranteed 20% return — better than any investmentCredit cards, personal loans, anything >6–7% APR
3. Emergency FundBuild 3–6 months of expenses in a high-yield savings accountWithout this buffer, any unexpected expense forces you into debt, derailing everything else3 months if stable job/income, 6 months if variable or self-employed
4. HSA (if eligible)Max your HSA if you have a qualifying high-deductible health planThe only triple-tax-advantaged account in existence. Tax-deductible going in, grows tax-free, tax-free coming out for medical expenses. After 65, works like a traditional IRA.$4,300 individual / $8,550 family (2025 limits)
5. Roth IRAMax your Roth IRATax-free growth forever. No RMDs. Can withdraw contributions (not gains) penalty-free anytime. Best account for someone young with low current income and long time horizon.$7,000/year (2025), income limit ~$161K single
6. Max 401kMax out the rest of your 401k beyond the matchPre-tax reduces your current tax bill; grows tax-deferred for decades$23,500/year (2025)
7. Taxable BrokerageInvest in a regular brokerage accountNo contribution limits, no withdrawal restrictions, but gains are taxableWhatever you have left after steps 1–6
8. Pay Off Low-Interest DebtExtra mortgage payments, student loans at <5%Low priority because expected investment returns likely exceed the interest rateOnly after steps 1–7 are handled
For a 20-year-old: The math is overwhelmingly in favor of getting money into tax-advantaged accounts early and leaving it alone. $7,000 in a Roth IRA at 22 (compounding at 8% for 43 years to age 65) = ~$185,000 tax-free. The same $7,000 invested at 40 = ~$30,000. Time is the asset.

What to invest in your 401k: Pick a target-date fund (e.g., "Vanguard Target Retirement 2065") if available — it automatically rebalances as you age. If not available, a simple 3-fund portfolio: US Total Market Index (60–70%), International Index (20–30%), Bond Index (0–10% when young). Lowest expense ratio options always win long-term.

Roth IRA — The Best Account You Have

FeatureDetails
Who can contributeAnyone with earned income below the phase-out: ~$146K–$161K single, ~$230K–$240K married (2025)
Contribution limit$7,000/year (2025); $8,000 if 50+. Must have earned income ≥ contribution amount.
Tax treatmentAfter-tax contributions. All growth and qualified withdrawals are 100% tax-free forever.
Withdrawal rulesContributions (not gains) can be withdrawn anytime, no penalty. Gains: tax/penalty-free after 59½ and account is 5+ years old.
No RMDsUnlike 401k and Traditional IRA, Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions. You can let it compound indefinitely.
Backdoor RothIf you exceed income limits: contribute to a Traditional IRA (non-deductible) then convert to Roth immediately. Legal and widely used.
Where to openFidelity, Vanguard, or Schwab. All free to open. Pick one and use it for decades.

HSA — The Hidden Triple-Tax Weapon

FeatureDetails
EligibilityMust be enrolled in a High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP). In 2025: deductible ≥ $1,650 individual / $3,300 family.
Triple tax advantage1. Contributions are tax-deductible. 2. Growth is tax-free. 3. Withdrawals for medical expenses are tax-free. No other account does all three.
2025 limits$4,300 individual / $8,550 family / $1,000 catch-up if 55+
The investing strategyDon't spend it. Pay medical expenses out of pocket (and save the receipts forever). Invest the HSA in index funds. After 65, it works exactly like a Traditional IRA — withdraw for anything, just pay ordinary income tax.
Receipt trickThere's no time limit on reimbursing yourself for past medical expenses. Pay a $500 doctor bill in 2025, save the receipt, reimburse yourself tax-free from the HSA in 2040. The money compounds in the meantime.
Where to holdFidelity HSA if your employer allows — zero fees and full investment options. Many employer-provided HSAs have high fees; rollover to Fidelity annually.

Emergency Fund — The Foundation

  • Target: 3–6 months of actual spending (not income)
  • Where: High-yield savings account (HYSA). In 2025, good HYSAs pay 4–5% APY. Try SoFi, Marcus by Goldman, Ally, or Discover Bank.
  • Do not invest this money. It's not an investment — it's insurance.
  • Keep it separate from your checking account so you can't accidentally spend it.

Budgeting — The Framework That Actually Works

The 50/30/20 Rule (Starting Point)

Category% of After-Tax IncomeWhat Goes Here
Needs50%Rent, utilities, groceries, minimum debt payments, insurance, transportation
Wants30%Dining out, entertainment, travel, subscriptions, clothes beyond basics
Savings/Investing20%Retirement accounts, emergency fund, debt paydown beyond minimums
In your 20s: Try to push savings above 20% — even 25–30% if your income allows. The difference between saving 20% vs. 30% over a career is enormous. Lifestyle creep is the enemy — when income rises, automatically increase your savings rate before you get used to the new spending level.

Zero-Based Budgeting (More Rigorous)

Every dollar of income gets assigned a job at the start of the month: savings, bills, food, fun, etc. Income minus all assigned categories = $0. Forces intentionality — you decide where money goes before you spend it.

Apps: YNAB (You Need A Budget) — $14/month, best-in-class. Free alternative: spreadsheet.

Pay Yourself First

Automatically move money to savings/investment accounts the day you get paid — before you can spend it. Then live on what's left. Easier than budgeting every line item.

Building Credit from Zero

  1. Get a secured credit card (Discover it Secured, Capital One Secured) — you deposit cash as collateral, get a card, use it for small purchases, pay in full monthly. Graduates to unsecured after 8–12 months.
  2. Or become an authorized user on a parent's card with good history. Their history shows up on your report immediately.
  3. Discover it Student or Chase Freedom Student — designed for students, no credit required, cash back.
  4. After 6–12 months of on-time payments: apply for a real rewards card (Chase Sapphire Preferred, etc.).
Free credit monitoring: Credit Karma (free, Equifax + TransUnion scores). Check your actual credit reports for free annually at annualcreditreport.com — the only official source mandated by federal law.

Taxes — The Basics You Need to Know

How Federal Income Tax Works (It's Not What You Think)

The US uses a progressive marginal tax system. Your tax rate does NOT apply to all your income — only to the income within each bracket. This is one of the most misunderstood things in personal finance.

2025 Tax Bracket (Single)Tax RateWhat It Means
$0 – $11,92510%Everyone pays 10% on their first $11,925 of taxable income
$11,926 – $48,47512%Only the income in this range is taxed at 12%
$48,476 – $103,35022%Only the income in this range is taxed at 22%
$103,351 – $197,30024%Only the income in this range is taxed at 24%
$197,301 – $250,52532%Only the income in this range is taxed at 32%
$250,526 – $626,35035%Only the income in this range is taxed at 35%
$626,351+37%Only income above this is taxed at 37%
Example: If you earn $60,000, you do NOT pay 22% on all $60,000. You pay 10% on the first $11,925, 12% on the next $36,550, and 22% only on the final $11,525. Your effective tax rate is much lower than your marginal rate.

Key Tax Concepts

ConceptWhat It IsWhy It Matters
Standard Deduction$15,000 single / $30,000 married (2025). Reduces your taxable income automatically.Most people take this — you only itemize if your deductions (mortgage interest, charitable gifts, state taxes) exceed it.
Taxable IncomeGross income − pre-tax deductions (401k, HSA, health insurance) − standard or itemized deductionThis is what you actually pay tax on — significantly lower than your gross income
W-2 vs. 1099W-2: employer withholds taxes. 1099: self-employed / contractor — you're responsible for quarterly estimated taxes and self-employment tax (15.3% on net income).Being a 1099 contractor looks like higher pay, but you're now paying the employer's share of Social Security/Medicare
Capital Gains TaxTax on investment profits. Short-term (held <1 year): taxed as ordinary income. Long-term (held >1 year): 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income.Never sell a winning investment within 12 months if you can help it. The tax difference is massive.
Tax-Loss HarvestingSelling investments at a loss to offset capital gains. Up to $3,000 in losses can offset ordinary income annually; remainder carries forward.A free tax reduction tool in any taxable brokerage account. Robo-advisors (Betterment, Wealthfront) do this automatically.
FICA TaxesSocial Security (6.2%) + Medicare (1.45%) = 7.65% of wages, matched by employer. Self-employed pay both sides = 15.3%.Not shown on your paycheck separately from income tax — you're paying this in addition to federal/state income tax

Tax Filing Basics

  • Deadline: April 15 each year (or next business day). Extensions extend to October 15 but don't delay tax owed — you must pay estimated taxes by April 15.
  • Where to file free: IRS Free File (freefile.irs.gov) if income <$84K. FreeTaxUSA is also excellent and nearly free.
  • Pay attention to: 1099-INT (bank interest), 1099-DIV (dividends), 1099-B (investment sales), 1099-G (unemployment). All need to be reported.
  • Student loan interest: Up to $2,500 deductible above-the-line even if you take the standard deduction.

Insurance — The Risk Transfer Basics

TypeWhat It CoversWhat to Know
Health InsuranceMedical expensesIf offered by employer, almost always take it even if it seems expensive. Understand your deductible (what you pay first), copay (fixed amount per visit), coinsurance (% you pay after deductible), and out-of-pocket maximum (your worst-case annual exposure). HDHP = lower premium + HSA eligibility.
Renter's InsuranceYour stuff + liability if someone is hurt in your placeCheapest insurance you'll buy — $10–20/month. Covers theft, fire, water damage to your belongings. Required by many landlords. No reason not to have it.
Auto InsuranceVehicle damage + liabilityLiability is the important part — protects you if you injure someone. Minimum state coverage is rarely enough. If your car is old and paid off, drop collision/comprehensive. Shop quotes with The Zebra or Policygenius annually.
Term Life InsurancePays a lump sum if you die during the termOnly needed if others depend on your income (future spouse, kids, co-signed debts). At 20 with no dependents: skip it. When you have dependents: 10–12x your income, 20–30 year term. Avoid whole/universal life — it's insurance + investing bundled badly.
Disability InsuranceReplaces income if you can't work due to illness/injuryUnderrated. More likely to be disabled than to die young. Employer often provides short-term; long-term disability is the one to get. 60% of income replacement is standard.
Umbrella InsuranceExtra liability coverage beyond auto/home limits$1M umbrella policy = $150–300/year. Worth it once you have meaningful assets. Covers you if you're sued for more than your auto/home policy limits.
